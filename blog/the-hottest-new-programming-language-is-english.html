The hottest new programming language is English.

Gita Projić

With everyone saying AI will replace all developers, and anyone who touches code for a living, it's hard not to spiral and start worrying about your future. Especially if you're an 18-year-old student who already goes to a vocational technical high school for it and plans on pursuing a CS degree.

With every model getting better and better, and a growing number of MCPs keep shrinking the list of things only humans can do. So you start to wonder what you're even trying for.

But, looking at history and the technology ACTUALLY implemented in today's society, I think we don't have to worry yet.

Every new technology kills some jobs and invents others to run and grow the new thing.

Look around and you'll spot the roles that used to be human. A traffic light used to be a person standing in the road directing cars. Phone calls used to be connected by hand: in the US there were around 342,000 switchboard operators at the peak in 1950, about 40,000 by 1984, and barely more than a thousand today. That work didn't vanish overnight, though. It faded over decades while demand for phones kept growing, and people moved into the jobs that grew up around it.

Furthermore, industries that aren't focused on tech are almost always behind on state-of-the-art technology. In Croatia, I am always amazed at how many tasks are still done manually at companies when they could benefit more from automating them.

Today, AI just removed another manual task: typing code, and what I think of as parametar work — digging through documentation to learn every flag, option, and compatibility quirk across all the different tools you're using.

"The hottest new programming language is English." Andrej Karpathy, the man who coined the term vibe coding, tweeted back in January 2023, and it stuck because it is basically true now.

So, as a student, I know what I will be focusing on: system design.

Knowing how everything fits together, infrastructure, security and improving how I use AI (so I'm not burning credits and I get the best result on the first try). And one of the most important, UI/UX, because only a human can come up with a genuinely original idea and test how a system feels to use. You have to know how to prompt all of these requirements, and terminology is key. It's how you and the model accomplish mutual understanding.

On top of that, knowing the concepts and the tools, and understanding the real pros and cons of each, helps the AI tremendously. If you know what you want and the best way to get there, there's far less room for error (and that's the way Anthropic recommends working in their courses). Besides, researching and going back and forth before writing code is part of the process.

So yes, English might be the hottest new programming language. But someone still has to know what to say, why to say it, and what "good" looks like when the code comes back. So that just proves education and experience are more important than ever, and we are lucky both are more accessible than ever! The manual part of the job is being automated, which leaves far more room for your creativity (I am not talking about generative art, yuck). The thinking part, the architecture, the taste, the judgment, is exactly what I'm going to spend my time getting good at. That's not a job AI is taking from me. It's the job it's freeing me up to do.
